# Case Study Anatomy

The canonical structure of a case study in this workspace. The angle stage maps the dossier onto these sections. The draft stage writes them. The polish stage checks they are all present and pulling weight.

The structure is built on one rule: **lead with the answer, then prove it.** Reviewers are time-constrained and often give a portfolio a five-minute first pass. State the outcome before the process so a skimmer gets the point immediately and chooses to read on.

---

## The Sections (outcome-first order)

A case study moves through five beats. The order is deliberately not chronological. It puts the result first (the Minto inversion: lead with the single strongest outcome, then the key decisions, then the supporting evidence).

### 1. Headline and Outcome

Open with the answer. A title that states the project's point (not just the product name), a one-line summary, your specific role, and the single strongest result up front -- a number if you have one, a concrete qualitative outcome if you do not. A reviewer should grasp what you did and why it mattered before reading anything else.

### 2. Problem

The problem or hypothesis in one clear sentence, then why it mattered and the constraints you worked inside. If you cannot state the problem in a single sentence, the case study is not ready. An unclear problem is the most common reason case studies fail.

### 3. Key Decisions

The heart of the case study, and the highest-leverage differentiator. The 2-3 decisions that produced the outcome. For each: the options you weighed, the reasoning, the choice, and the tradeoff. Include at least one path you rejected and why. Showing candidate solutions left on the cutting-room floor signals real judgment. This is the section most portfolios undercook.

### 4. Evidence and Impact

How you know it worked. The chain of evidence behind the headline result, drawn from the impact toolkit (see `impact-evidence.md`): before/after, proxy indicators, quotes, decision-influence. If the outcome was never measured, say what you would measure and why. Be honest about mixed results.

### 5. Reflection

Two to four sentences. One honest thing you learned, framed forward. No tidy morals.

---

## The NN/G Content Checklist

Across the five sections above, every case study should answer these seven, in scannable form:

1. The problem or hypothesis
2. Your specific role and how you collaborated
3. How you arrived at the solution
4. How the solution solved the problem
5. Challenges faced, including design concepts you did NOT pursue
6. How the project affected users and the business
7. What you learned

## Length and Scannability

Target length: {{TARGET_LENGTH}}. As a rule, 500-800 words or 10-12 slides for one project and one core problem.

Design the case study like a product, for scanning:

- **Headlines as signposts.** A reviewer who reads only the headlines should understand the whole story. Test it.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Bold the key terms. Generous white space.
- **Show and tell.** Pair each visual with a caption that explains why it mattered, not what it is.
- Curate key screens. A gallery of every screen reads as a gallery, not a case study.

---

## What Every Case Study Must Have

- The problem, stated in one sentence, in your own words
- The outcome stated up front, before the process
- At least one key decision shown with its reasoning and tradeoff
- At least one path you rejected, and why
- A chain of evidence for the outcome, or an honest statement of what you would measure
- A visible sense of your specific role and contribution
- One honest reflection or limitation
