## General Rules

- Lead every section with its most important sentence.
- Show decisions, not just deliverables. A screenshot proves you made something; the reasoning proves you should be hired.
- Prefer the specific over the abstract. "Cut the onboarding from nine steps to four" beats "streamlined onboarding."
- Write in past tense, first person, owning your contribution while crediting the team honestly.

## Section by Section

### Context

Three to five sentences. Product, team, your role, timeframe, the one constraint that matters most. Enough for the reader to follow the decisions, no more.

### Challenge

State the real problem in your own words and make it a question the reader wants answered. Resist restating the brief. The gap between the brief and the real problem is often the most interesting part.

### Approach

The longest section. Walk through the key decisions in the order they mattered. For each: the options, the reasoning, the choice, and the tradeoff. Include at least one dead end. This is where REVEALS-THINKING and DEMONSTRATES-CRAFT are won.

### Outcome

Open with the headline result. Then the evidence behind it. Then the honest texture: what was mixed, what you would still fix. Specific numbers if you have them; concrete observed change if you do not.

### Reflection

Two to four sentences. One honest thing you learned, framed forward. Avoid tidy morals.

## Visual Evidence

Mark images inline as bracketed notes, for example `[diagram: the three states of the form]`. Each visual should show a decision or a result. Do not invent visuals that do not exist.
